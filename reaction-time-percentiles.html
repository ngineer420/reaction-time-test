Reaction Time Percentiles: What Your Score Actually Means

"Is 250ms good?" is the question every reaction time test creates and almost none of them answer. This page answers it with a percentile table for simple visual reaction time — the exact thing the test on this site measures — and shows where every number in it came from.

Where these numbers come from. They are a model fitted to figures published in the scientific literature and in one large public web dataset, all cited at the bottom of this page. They are not a record of scores on this site. ReflexZap has no backend and no database: your results are held in your own browser and are never uploaded, so there is nothing here to aggregate. Any site that claims to rank you against "other visitors" without an account system is telling you a story.

The distribution

Simple visual reaction time is not symmetrical. There is a hard floor — signal has to cross the retina, the optic nerve, the cortex and the motor pathway, and that takes time no matter who you are — but no ceiling, because attention can always wander. The result is a right-skewed curve with a long slow tail, which is why the median sits below the mean and why "average reaction time" quoted as a single number is a slightly misleading thing to quote.

These bands describe a session average over five rounds, which is a steadier number than any single click. One round in isolation swings by tens of milliseconds purely from moment-to-moment attention: Woods and colleagues measured a mean trial-to-trial standard deviation of 40.0ms within each individual, which is larger than the 26.8ms standard deviation between individuals. In other words, the spread inside one person's own results is wider than the spread between people. That is the single most useful thing to know about reaction time scores, and it is why one bad round is not evidence of anything.

Reaction time by age

Simple visual reaction time slows gradually and steadily through adult life. In the largest of the studies cited below (1,469 adults aged 18–65, 120 scored trials each), mean reaction time rose by 0.55ms per year of age — around five and a half milliseconds a decade.

The lab mean column is printed exactly as published. The typical browser test column adds 42ms to each — the gap between that study's overall mean of 231ms and the 273ms median of the 81-million-click browser dataset also cited below. It is an adjustment, not a measurement, and it is there so you can compare your own browser score against your age band without being misled by a number collected on laboratory equipment.

Two things worth noticing. The change across a lifetime is real but small — about 21ms separates the youngest band from the oldest, which is less than the difference a 60Hz monitor makes on its own. And the spread within each band grows with age (the published standard deviations run from 19.5ms in the youngest band to 28.1ms in the oldest), so age predicts variability better than it predicts speed.

How much of your score is your equipment?

This is the part almost nobody covers, and it matters more than most of the advice you will find about improving your reflexes. A browser test does not measure your reaction time. It measures your reaction time plus the time your computer takes to show you the cue, plus the time it takes to notice your click. Both are real milliseconds and neither is yours.

16.7ms
One frame at 60Hz
A 60Hz screen only redraws 60 times a second. The cue can be ready and still wait up to a full frame to appear — 8.3ms on average, 16.7ms at worst, before the panel has even started changing colour.
6.9ms
One frame at 144Hz
The same wait on a 144Hz gaming monitor. Switching from 60Hz to 144Hz hands back around 5ms of average delay for free — without you getting any faster.
8ms
125Hz mouse polling
A standard mouse reports its state 125 times a second, so a click can sit finished-but-unreported for up to 8ms. A 1000Hz gaming mouse cuts that to 1ms.
17.8ms
Even in the lab
Woods and colleagues measured their own research rig with a photodiode: 11.0ms from the monitor and 6.8ms from the response button. Their headline 231ms mean becomes 213ms once that is removed.
~30ms
A typical desktop
The figure Human Benchmark publishes alongside its 81-million-click dataset as the typical lag of a desktop or laptop today. It notes recorded times have drifted slower over the years as operating systems added visual effects.
71–87ms
Online experiment platforms
Anwyl-Irvine and colleagues drove four research-grade browser platforms with a calibrated robot finger and measured mean response lags in this range — varying by browser (Safari 76.5ms to Firefox 82.3ms) and by system (Windows laptop 73.7ms to macOS desktop 85.4ms).

Stack those up and the practical consequence is blunt: most of the distance between a "good" score and an "average" one is equipment. A 60Hz laptop with a trackpad and a busy operating system can easily read 40–50ms slower than a 144Hz monitor with a 1000Hz wired mouse, for exactly the same person on exactly the same day. That is the whole gap between the 229ms of the fastest tenth and the 273ms median.

Three things follow from that, and they are the reason this page exists:

  • Compare yourself to yourself. Your own trend on your own hardware is a real measurement. Your rank against a modelled population on unknown hardware is entertainment with a citation attached.
  • Chase the hardware before you chase the reflexes. A faster display and a wired mouse will move your number further than any amount of practice, because practice cannot make the frame arrive sooner.
  • Distrust very fast scores, including your own. Anything under about 198ms on a browser test is at the edge of the physically possible, and the most common explanation is a click that anticipated the cue rather than reacted to it.

Methodology

The model has two inputs and no free parameters. Both are published figures, cited below, and the arithmetic between them is shown here so you can check it. The whole thing lives in assets/js/percentile.js on this site, with the same derivation in a comment at the top of the file.

What is being modelled. Not a single click, but one person's average across the five rounds this site runs — because that is the number you are given and therefore the number that has to be placed on a curve.

Where the middle comes from. The median of 273ms is Human Benchmark's published figure across more than 81 million reaction time clicks. It is the right anchor because it is the same task measured through the same medium: a browser, on whatever hardware people happen to own. Their mean of 284ms is deliberately not used — a raw single-click dataset carries a long tail of distracted clicks and mistimed taps that drags a mean upwards, and the median ignores it.

Where the spread comes from. No large web dataset publishes one, so it is derived from Woods and colleagues, who report both components separately. The standard deviation between people's average reaction times is 26.8ms. The trial-to-trial standard deviation within one person is 40.0ms, so averaging just five rounds leaves 40.0 ÷ √5 = 17.9ms of sampling noise on top. Combining them gives √(26.8² + 17.9²) = 32.2ms for the spread of a five-round average, which is 32.2 ÷ 231 = 13.9% of that study's own mean. Applied to the 273ms browser-scale median: 38ms.

The shape. A lognormal, pinned by exactly those two numbers. Reaction time is bounded below and unbounded above — nothing gets you under the transit time of your own nervous system, but anything can make a round slower — so the curve has to be right-skewed, and a lognormal is the standard choice for that. Nothing in it is fitted, tuned or guessed.

What this model is not. It is not a measurement of this site's visitors: there is no backend, no account system and no database here, and your results never leave your browser. It is not a clinical instrument, and it should not be used as one. And it is not precise to the millisecond — it is a smooth curve drawn through published summary statistics, which is why the percentile shown on your results is capped at 1 and 99 rather than pretending the tails are known.
