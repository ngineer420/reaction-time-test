Audio vs visual reaction time: why sound wins

Every study that measures both stimuli in the same people finds the same ordering: a person responds to a sound faster than to a light. The reason is not that hearing is "more alert" — it is that the two senses turn a stimulus into a nerve signal by completely different machinery, and one of those machines is roughly a thousand times faster than the other.

Your four tests checking…

This site runs four reaction tests, each scored against its own published distribution. They are stored separately in this browser and none of them is uploaded.

The mechanism: a channel versus a cascade

A hair cell in the cochlea is a mechanical device. Sound bends its bundle of stereocilia, the bend pulls directly on the filaments linking one stereocilium to the next, and that pull yanks open an ion channel. There is no messenger in between — the stimulus is the thing opening the gate. Corey and Hudspeth measured how long that takes in bullfrog saccular hair cells and found a delay of about 40 microseconds at 22 °C, noting that a latency that short, and that insensitive to temperature, rules out an enzymatic step entirely. Forty microseconds is 0.04 of a millisecond.

A photoreceptor is a chemical amplifier. A photon isomerises a molecule of retinal inside rhodopsin; activated rhodopsin catalyses the exchange of GTP onto hundreds of copies of the G protein transducin; each transducin activates a phosphodiesterase; the phosphodiesterase hydrolyses cyclic GMP; and only when enough cGMP has disappeared does the channel — which sits open in the dark — finally close. Every one of those steps is a diffusion-limited chemical reaction with its own time constant. Schnapf and colleagues recorded the photocurrent of single primate cones and found the flash response behaved like a bandpass filter peaking near 5 Hz: tens of milliseconds to rise and fall, not microseconds.

That is the whole answer in one line. The ear opens a gate; the eye runs a chemical reaction. The cascade buys the eye extraordinary sensitivity — a rod can signal a single photon — and it pays for it in time.

You can watch the difference downstream

The gap is not a theoretical inference. It shows up in the two standard clinical recordings of the sensory pathways, which are measured in the same units on the same kind of equipment:

~10ms
Auditory brainstem response
The whole series of far-field waves Jewett and Williston first averaged from the human scalp has run its course within about ten milliseconds of the click.
~100ms
Visual evoked potential
The principal component of the clinical pattern-reversal VEP is called P100 because its positive peak lands near 100 ms after the reversal.

An order of magnitude separates them. Some of that is the extra cortical processing a pattern needs, not transduction alone — but the direction and the scale both follow the machinery.

Why your score only moves by about twenty milliseconds

Here is the part most explanations skip. If the front end of hearing is a thousand times faster than the front end of vision, why is the behavioural difference so small?

Because transduction is a small slice of a reaction. Pressing a button after a stimulus is transduction, then conduction, then a decision, then motor planning, then the muscle actually contracting, then — in a browser test — the mouse's own polling interval and the display's next refresh. Nearly all of that is shared between the two senses. Only the first slice differs, so only the first slice can show up in the total. A stimulus arriving 20 ms earlier at the cortex produces a response about 20 ms earlier, on top of 200-odd milliseconds that are identical either way.

That is what the measured contrast looks like: about 20 ms, in the same people, on the same apparatus. This site's audio model is built by shifting the visual model by exactly that figure, and the full derivation — the study, its 120 subjects, its fastest-of-five scoring rule and why only the contrast rather than the absolute levels transfers — is set out on the percentiles page: how the audio model is derived.

Be suspicious of the much larger gaps quoted elsewhere. Forty and fifty millisecond differences generally come from setting one study's visual figure beside a different study's auditory figure — which measures the difference between two laboratories as well as between two senses.

Reading your saved results…

Both figures above are the mean of your recent session averages — a mean of 5-round means on each side, so like is compared with like. This page deliberately does not compare your single fastest round on one test against your single fastest on the other: a fastest-of-many figure is a different statistic, it drifts upward with every extra round you play, and setting it beside a published mean-based contrast would be an apples-to-oranges comparison dressed up as a personal result. Nothing here is uploaded, merged into one profile, or scored — it is your own two records, read and displayed.

What it is good for

Three places the difference is load-bearing, and one where it is not.

Starting signals are sounds for a reason. A starting pistol, a beep, a countdown tone — all of them beat a light on average, which is why the events that care most about start latency use them. When a light is the signal, as at an F1 start, the whole field is on the slower channel together.

In games, audio cues are the early warning. Footsteps and reloads reach you before a sprite does, which is part of why competitive players spend more on headphones than the frame rate alone would justify. The caveat is that this only holds for audio that arrives promptly: Bluetooth adds latency measured in tens of milliseconds and can wipe out the entire physiological advantage.

Alarms in noisy rooms are a trade-off, not a free win. The auditory advantage is real, but it is small next to the cost of missing a signal entirely because a room is loud or a listener has their back turned — which is why safety-critical systems tend to use both channels rather than picking the faster one.

It will not move your percentile much. Twenty milliseconds is a couple of tiers on this site's scale and less than the difference between a 60 Hz monitor and a 144 Hz one. If you are chasing a number, the equipment is still the bigger lever — that is what the latency breakdown is for.

Sources for the mechanism

Every figure on this page traces to one of these. The percentile models the tests are scored against have their own, separate sources, listed in full on the percentiles page.

  • Corey DP, Hudspeth AJ (1979). Response latency of vertebrate hair cells. Biophysical Journal 26(3):499-506.peer-reviewed
    https://doi.org/10.1016/S0006-3495(79)85267-4
    Bullfrog saccular hair cells, in vitro. Corrected for the electrical time constant of the epithelium, the transepithelial microphonic response follows a fast mechanical stimulus with a 40-microsecond delay at 22 degrees C. The authors note that the short latency and its modest temperature dependence limit the possible models for hair-cell transduction — i.e. it is too fast to be an enzymatic cascade.
  • Schnapf JL, Nunn BJ, Meister M, Baylor DA (1990). Visual transduction in cones of the monkey Macaca fascicularis. Journal of Physiology 427:681-713.peer-reviewed
    https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.1990.sp018193
    Membrane current of single primate cone outer segments. The response to a brief flash is diphasic and resembles the output of a bandpass filter with a peak frequency near 5 Hz — that is, the photocurrent takes tens of milliseconds to rise and fall, not microseconds.
  • Jewett DL, Williston JS (1971). Auditory-evoked far fields averaged from the scalp of humans. Brain 94(4):681-696.peer-reviewed
    https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/94.4.681
    The paper that established the auditory brainstem response: far-field potentials averaged from the human scalp, whose series of waves runs its course within roughly the first ten milliseconds after a click.
  • Odom JV, Bach M, Brigell M, Holder GE, McCulloch DL, Mizota A, Tormene AP; International Society for Clinical Electrophysiology of Vision (2016). ISCEV standard for clinical visual evoked potentials: (2016 update). Documenta Ophthalmologica 133(1):1-9.clinical standard
    https://doi.org/10.1007/s10633-016-9553-y
    The clinical standard for pattern-reversal visual evoked potentials, whose principal component is named P100 for the positive peak that lands near 100 ms after the reversal.

The two transduction figures are measurements in animal preparations — a bullfrog sacculus and a macaque retina — because single-cell recordings cannot be made in a conscious human. They are quoted for the mechanism and its scale, not as human latencies. The evoked-potential figures above them are human, and they are the reason the mechanism's direction is not in doubt.
